# P/E vs PEG Ratio

The P/E ratio tells you how many years of current earnings the market is paying for a stock; the PEG ratio divides that by expected earnings growth, so companies growing at different speeds can be compared on one scale.

  - Explain what a P/E implies about growth and required return using the Gordon growth link

  - Recognize when a low P/E is a value trap and when a high PEG is justified

## Why It Matters

<div class="audience-beginner" markdown="1">

If a shop earns 10 a year and someone sells it to you for 200, you are paying 20 times its earnings. That is the P/E. On its own it does not tell you much: paying 20 times for a shop whose profits double every few years is a bargain; paying 20 times for one that is shrinking is expensive. The PEG ratio adds growth into the picture.

</div>

<div class="audience-analyst" markdown="1">

P/E is a compressed DCF. In a steady-state model, $$P/E = \frac{\text{payout ratio}}{r - g}$$ (on forward earnings), and since payout = 1 - g / ROE, the multiple depends on required return, growth and the return on reinvested capital together. PEG is a heuristic that linearizes this relationship. It is useful for screening but ignores ROIC, duration of growth and risk.

</div>

## Formulas

$$
\text{P/E} = \frac{\text{Share price}}{\text{Earnings per share}} = \frac{\text{Market capitalization}}{\text{Net income}}
$$

| Variant | Earnings used | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trailing (TTM) P/E | Last 12 months reported EPS | Real, audited numbers | Backward-looking |
| Forward P/E | Next 12 months consensus EPS | Reflects expected change | Analyst estimates are often optimistic |
| Shiller CAPE | 10-year average inflation-adjusted EPS | Smooths the cycle | Slow; affected by accounting changes |

$$
\text{PEG} = \frac{\text{P/E}}{\text{Expected annual EPS growth (in \%)}}
$$

A P/E of 30 with 20% expected growth gives a PEG of 1.5. By Peter Lynch's rule of thumb, PEG near 1 is fair, below 1 is potentially cheap, and above 2 is expensive. Use growth over the next 3-5 years, and say which P/E (trailing or forward) you used.

**Dividend-adjusted PEG** (for mature payers): $$\text{PEGY} = \frac{\text{P/E}}{g + \text{dividend yield}}$$.

## What a P/E Implies

The inverse of P/E is the **earnings yield**. For a no-growth company that pays out everything, earnings yield ≈ required return. With growth, the Gordon growth model gives:

$$
\frac{P}{E_1} = \frac{1 - g/\text{ROE}}{r - g}
$$

**Example.** Required return 10%, growth 6%, ROE 20%: payout = 1 - 6/20 = 70%, so fair forward P/E = 0.70 / 0.04 = **17.5x**. If ROE were only 8%, payout = 25% and fair P/E = 0.25 / 0.04 = **6.25x** - same growth, much lower value, because low-ROE growth consumes most of the earnings.

## Sector and Market Benchmarks

Ranges below are rough, through-the-cycle forward P/E guides. Compare a company with its own 5-10 year history and close peers first.

| Sector | US forward P/E | India forward P/E | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broad market | S&P 500: 15-22x | Nifty 50: 17-24x | India has traded at a structural premium to other emerging markets |
| Software / internet | 25-45x | 30-60x | Use EV/Sales or Rule of 40 for loss-makers |
| Consumer staples / FMCG | 18-25x | 40-60x | Indian FMCG trades at some of the highest multiples globally |
| Banks | 9-14x | 12-20x (private); 6-10x (PSU) | Use P/B and ROE together |
| Industrials | 15-22x | 25-45x | Cyclical - use mid-cycle earnings |
| Energy / materials | 8-14x | 8-15x | P/E is lowest at the earnings peak - a trap |
| Utilities | 14-19x | 12-20x | Rate-sensitive |

## Red Flags

- **Low P/E on peak earnings.** Cyclical companies (steel, chemicals, shipping, auto) look cheapest at the top of the cycle, just before earnings fall.
- **Earnings inflated by one-offs** - asset sales, tax credits, lower provisions. Use adjusted EPS carefully and read the notes.
- **Forward P/E far below trailing P/E** because of heroic estimates.
- **PEG built on one year of growth** or on growth from a depressed base.
- **High P/E and falling ROIC.** The market is paying for growth that may not create value.
- **Share count rising.** EPS growth below net income growth means dilution (see [Revenue vs EPS CAGR](08-Revenue-vs-EPS-CAGR.mdx)).

## Case Studies

**US - Nvidia, 2023-2024.** Nvidia's trailing P/E exceeded 100x in early 2023, which looked absurd. But EPS grew several-fold over the next year on data-centre demand, and its forward P/E fell into the 30-50x range even as the share price rose. A high trailing P/E can hide a low forward PEG; the question is whether the growth is durable.

**India - Hindustan Unilever vs PSU banks.** For years, Hindustan Unilever traded at 50-70x earnings with high-single to low-double digit growth - a PEG well above 3 - while large public sector banks traded at 5-8x earnings. HUL's multiple reflects very high ROCE, low capital needs, predictability and a long growth runway; PSU banks' multiples reflected asset-quality risk and lower returns on equity. Neither multiple is "wrong" without reference to ROIC and risk.

## Check Yourself

```quiz
- q: "A stock trades at 45 with EPS of 3.0 and expected EPS growth of 18%. What is its PEG?"
  options: ["0.83", "15", "1.2", "2.7"]
  answer: 1
  explain: "P/E = 45 / 3 = 15. PEG = 15 / 18 = 0.83."
- q: "Why can a cyclical stock look cheapest on P/E at exactly the wrong time?"
  options: ["P/E ignores the share price", "At the cycle peak, earnings are unusually high, making the P/E unusually low just before earnings fall", "Cyclicals never pay dividends", "Accounting rules change at the peak"]
  answer: 2
  explain: "Peak earnings shrink the P/E. When the cycle turns, earnings fall and the stock often falls with them."
- q: "Two companies both grow EPS at 8%. Company A has ROE of 25%, Company B has ROE of 10%. Which deserves the higher P/E, all else equal?"
  options: ["A", "B", "Neither"]
  answer: 1
  explain: "A needs to retain only 8/25 = 32% of earnings to grow, and can pay out the rest. B must retain 80%. Higher payout at the same growth means higher value."
- q: "What does a P/E of 25 mean in terms of earnings yield, and how does that compare with a 7% government bond?"
  answer: "Earnings yield = 1/25 = 4%. It is below the 7% bond yield, so investors are relying on earnings growth to make the stock worthwhile compared with the risk-free bond."
```

## Exercises

```exercise
- title: Implied fair P/E
  task: |
    A company has ROE of 18%, expected long-run growth of 7%, and a cost of equity of 11.5%. Calculate its justified forward P/E. Then recompute it for growth of 9%. What does the change tell you?
  solution: |
    At g = 7%: payout = 1 - 7/18 = 61.1%. P/E = 0.611 / 0.045 = **13.6x**.
    At g = 9%: payout = 1 - 9/18 = 50%. P/E = 0.50 / 0.025 = **20x**.
    Two points of extra growth raised the justified P/E by nearly 50%, because the denominator (r - g) shrinks - the reason high-multiple stocks are so sensitive to growth disappointments.
- title: PEG screen sanity check
  task: |
    Run a screen for PEG under 1 in your market (see [Stock Screening](../../10-Stock-Screening/INDEX.mdx)). For the top five results, check: Is the growth estimate from one year or several? Are earnings near a cyclical peak? Is ROIC above WACC? Remove any that fail and explain why.
```

## Study Notes

- **P/E = Price / EPS**; state trailing or forward.
- **PEG = P/E / growth %**; about 1 is "fair" by convention - a heuristic, not a law.
- Justified P/E = (1 - g/ROE) / (r - g): growth only adds value with high ROE/ROIC.
- Cyclicals: low P/E at the peak is a trap; use mid-cycle earnings.
- Compare with sector and own history; Indian multiples run structurally higher in many sectors.
